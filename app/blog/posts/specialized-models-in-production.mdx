It's the first Saturday of October, and I'm driving to a birthday party at a farm in Maryland. There are goats to feed, rabbits to see, tractor rides, and a zip line.

On the drive, I keep thinking about something that comes up in conversations with friends and on AI podcasts: enterprises want to depend less on frontier models. The usual alternative is "use open-source models and host them yourself."

I don't disagree with reducing that dependence. But I still have three questions: which model, doing what work, and deployed where?

Before I get out of the car, I record a voice note with those questions and how I'd explain them to someone else. That note is where this post started.

To answer them, I did some market research: which companies have published about adapting a model for one job and putting it in a product, and what results did they get? Here are eight, from extracting a customer's issue to reviewing a payment for fraud. The numbers come from their own reports, and Harvey's newer work is still research.

If you're considering fine-tuning for your own product, these examples can help you pick which tasks to test and how to compare the results with what you use today.

## A few terms before the company names

| Term | What it means here |
| ---- | ------------------ |
| Frontier model | A leading general-purpose model, used as a starting point or comparison |
| Open-weight model | A model whose learned parameters are available (its license and training-data disclosure are separate questions) |
| LLM | Large language model, the kind that generates text |
| GPU | The processor used to train and run models |
| Fine-tuning (post-training) | Adapting an existing model with more training for the behavior you need |
| Inference (serving) | Running the trained model to produce an answer |
| Evaluation | Checking outputs against examples and criteria that define success |

Open weights also don't mean the company releases its adapted model or owns the machines serving it.

First, though, you need a job small enough to describe.

## 1. Intercom: finding the customer's issue

<DiagramViewer
  company="Intercom"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/intercom-editorial.webp"
  alt="Intercom previously used one model call to detect and extract a customer issue. ModernBERT now detects whether there is an issue; if so, LoRA-tuned Qwen3-14B extracts it for retrieval, reranking, and a Fin answer."
  width={3072}
  height={2048}
/>

**The problem.** Before Fin can answer a support question, it needs a clear version of what the customer is asking. A conversation can include a correction, a greeting, and a reply to an earlier answer, and searching on all of that at once makes the next step harder.

**What they did.** Intercom adapted Qwen3-14B to pull out the customer's issue, using LoRA (a method that trains a small set of changes to the model). Its [September 2025 report](https://fin.ai/research/david-vs-goliath-are-small-llms-any-good/) describes 60,000 training examples from consenting customers, with personal information redacted.

Later, a custom model called Apex became the core answering model for nearly all English chat and email conversations, replacing Sonnet 4.0, according to its [March 2026 announcement](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-fin-apex-the-age-of-vertical-models-is-here/). [Fergal Reid's account](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/announcing-fin-apex-custom-llm-powering-fergal-reid-phd--6a2zf/) says it started from an unnamed open-weight model, runs on EC2 for more than a million conversations a week, and still hands some work, like Procedures, to frontier models such as Sonnet.

**The result.** The pipeline's offline answer rate reaches 63.4%, against 63.3% with GPT-4.1 and 18% with untuned Qwen. In a one-week production test, transaction cost falls 12.5%, answer rate falls 0.1 percentage points, and customer satisfaction stays unchanged. Median latency gets 100 milliseconds worse.

Training only on clearly resolved conversations hurt the answer rate, because the model became reluctant to extract an issue from an ambiguous one. My takeaway is that what goes into the training examples matters as much as which model you pick. Shopify builds its whole process around that.

## 2. Shopify: classifying products

<DiagramViewer
  company="Shopify"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/shopify-editorial.webp"
  alt="Shopify uses a fine-tuned Qwen2-VL 7B model to turn product text and images into requested product fields, with reviewed examples feeding training."
  width={3072}
  height={1536}
/>

**The problem.** A merchant's title, description, and images each hold part of what Shopify needs to classify a product. The output still has to fit consistent product types and attributes, even when the input doesn't.

**What they did.** Shopify's [July 2025 catalogue report](https://shopify.engineering/leveraging-multimodal-llms) describes fine-tuned models that read text and images, including Qwen2VL 7B, handling 40 million calls and roughly 16 billion tokens a day (tokens are the pieces of text models process). Several models produce labels, an arbitrator reconciles them, and people review the evaluation labels. Uncertain predictions and production failures feed the next training round. Shopify also trained the models to write only the fields that need filling.

**The result.** Median latency drops from two seconds to 500 milliseconds, and GPU usage falls 40%. The report credits the whole training-and-serving change, not fine-tuning alone.

Sidekick, Shopify's GraphQL agent, uses the same idea, according to its [August 2026 report](https://shopify.engineering/sidekicks-continual-learning-loop). It writes and runs queries against a merchant's store data (GraphQL is the query language for that). Low-scoring conversations become repair candidates, frontier models propose fixes, an automated evaluator checked against human judgments scores them, and successful sequences of actions become training examples.

Shopify estimates about $1 million a year to serve the specialized model, against $27 million for frontier inference at that traffic. Those are projections, and they don't fully account for training and maintaining the loop. Sidekick's base model is unnamed, so the catalogue is the clearer open-weight example.

## 3. Airbnb: notes for support agents

<DiagramViewer
  company="Airbnb"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/airbnb-editorial.webp"
  alt="Airbnb moved from bulk summaries to progressive notes: Mixtral generates notes, DeBERTa filters trivial ones, and a support agent uses the remaining notes."
  width={3072}
  height={1536}
/>

**The problem.** Support agents need to pick up a conversation without reading every earlier message and call transcript. A summary at the end helps the next person, but notes that stay current can help the person already handling the case.

**What they did.** Airbnb's [2025 paper](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-industry.140.pdf) describes a Mixtral-8×7B model that writes incremental notes as the conversation goes, with a separate classifier that filters out trivial bullets. It was trained on about 30,000 examples, then about 8,000 more drawn from agent edits. It runs on two A100 GPUs per instance, with median latency of 600 milliseconds.

**The result.** Handling time drops 3%, and up to 9% for cases involving three or more agents. That's measured against sites already using bulk summaries from a fine-tuned Mixtral model. The gain belongs to the whole progressive-note workflow (timing, filtering, prompts, and the model), and the study doesn't separate them.

## 4. LinkedIn: assessing candidates

<DiagramViewer
  company="LinkedIn"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/linkedin-editorial.webp"
  alt="Candidate profiles, recruiter notes and job requirements feed on-premises EON-8B, adapted from Llama 3.1 8B, which returns an explained match assessment to a recruiter."
  width={3072}
  height={2048}
/>

**The problem.** LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant keeps repeating one step: assessing a candidate against a role. That step accounts for nearly 90% of its LLM calls.

**What they did.** LinkedIn adapted Llama 3.1 8B into EON-8B, trained and served on-premises, according to its [December 18, 2024 report](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/generative-ai/how-we-built-domain-adapted-foundation-genai-models-to-power-our-platform). It reads candidate information, recruiter notes, and job requirements, then returns an assessment with an explanation.

**The result.** Matching accuracy is four percentage points higher than GPT-4o mini without additional fine-tuning on the matching task. That's a dated result on a matching test, not evidence of better hiring outcomes.

## 5. Coinbase: catching fraud

<DiagramViewer
  company="Coinbase"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/coinbase-editorial.webp"
  alt="Existing machine-learning models and rules block some transactions; selected transactions go to tuned Qwen3.5-9B for risk classification before application policy is applied. Existing blocks stand."
  width={3072}
  height={2048}
/>

**The problem.** Coinbase wanted to catch more fraud in Onramp, its product for buying crypto through partner apps, on top of its existing machine-learning models and rules.

**What they did.** [Brian Armstrong's October 11 post](https://x.com/brian_armstrong/status/2109412046244913643) describes fine-tuning Qwen3.5-9B on Coinbase's internal data. The engineering team's October 6 to 8 series shows how they got there:

- [Part one](https://www.coinbase.com/blog/part-one-how-coinbase-built-evaluated-and-post-trained-a-fraud-agent): A Claude Opus agent reviews selected transactions after the existing models and rules. It can flag more risk, but it can't override an upstream block. In the online experiment, fraudulent transactions fall 30% and fraud value falls 22% compared with the existing stack alone.
- [Part two](https://www.coinbase.com/blog/part-two-owning-intelligence-series): Coinbase tests models on 16,140 historical transactions across 7,293 users, including 813 confirmed fraud cases, all from before the agent rollout. Newer versions of Opus, Sonnet, and GPT have lower recall and F1 on this benchmark.
- [Part three](https://www.coinbase.com/blog/part-three-owning-intelligence-series): Coinbase adapts Qwen3.5-9B with reinforcement learning (GRPO, with LoRA updates), checking rewards against known fraud outcomes. It serves the model with Ray Serve LLM and vLLM on autoscaled L40S GPUs.

**The result.** Against Opus 4.5, Coinbase reports gains of 7.5 percentage points in precision, 12.0 in recall, 9.6 in F1, and 35.4 in dollar-weighted recall. Recall is how much fraud the model catches, precision is how many of its flags are right, F1 combines the two, and dollar-weighted recall is the share of fraudulent transaction value identified.

Median end-to-end LLM-request latency is 0.683 seconds against 1.515 seconds for Opus 4.5, a 55% reduction. Detection quality and latency come from separate evaluations, and the report doesn't isolate a live fraud-loss reduction from replacing Opus with tuned Qwen.

## 6. Impel: writing dealership replies

<DiagramViewer
  company="Impel"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/impel-editorial.webp"
  alt="Impel replaced a third-party model with LoRA-tuned Llama on SageMaker in its AWS account to produce personalized replies, conversation summaries and follow-ups."
  width={3072}
  height={2048}
/>

**The problem.** Impel makes Sales AI for dealerships, including replies, follow-ups, and conversation summaries, and it was running on a third-party model.

**What they did.** According to its [June 2025 account with AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/impel-enhances-automotive-dealership-customer-experience-with-fine-tuned-llms-on-amazon-sagemaker/), Impel replaced that model with a LoRA-adapted Llama model on autoscaled SageMaker endpoints in its own AWS account.

**The result.** Human-review scores rise from 73% to 86% for personalized replies, 70% to 83% for summaries, and 59% to 92% for follow-ups. The report leaves out the exact Llama variant, the sample size, and the scoring rules, and it doesn't measure vehicle-sales lift or cost savings.

## 7. Cresta: suggesting answers to support agents

<DiagramViewer
  company="Cresta"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/cresta-editorial.webp"
  alt="Live conversations and retrieved knowledge feed Mistral-based Ocean with customer LoRA on Fireworks-managed serving; the suggested answer goes to a human agent."
  width={3072}
  height={2048}
/>

**The problem.** Human agents in live support conversations need a good suggested answer quickly. Cresta's Knowledge Assist builds one from the conversation and retrieved company knowledge.

**What they did.** Cresta uses tuned Mistral models, adapted by industry, customer, and task with its Ocean training framework, according to its [engineering report](https://cresta.com/blog/how-fine-tuned-llms-power-knowledge-assist-summarization-and-chat-suggestions). [Fireworks' deployment account](https://fireworks.ai/blog/story-cresta-knowledge-assist) describes a shared base model with customer-specific LoRA adapters on provider-managed dedicated instances. Cresta customizes the models, and Fireworks operates the serving infrastructure.

**The result.** The tuned models are live with customers.

The setups differ even where the idea is the same. Impel deploys in its own AWS account, Cresta uses a serving partner, and LinkedIn describes on-premises serving. All three adapt models, and the infrastructure responsibilities differ.

## 8. Harvey: multi-step legal work

<DiagramViewer
  company="Harvey"
  src="/blog/specialized-models-in-production/harvey-editorial.webp"
  alt="Harvey research on diligence: fixed reader sub-agents examine legal documents and return findings to a Qwen3.5-122B-A10B root agent, which coordinates them and assembles a diligence memo."
  width={3072}
  height={2048}
/>

**The problem.** Legal work often takes many steps across many documents, from answering questions across a stack of contracts to reviewing a whole document room.

**What they did.** Harvey post-trained open-weight models for specific legal tasks, in three efforts:

- [Tenet](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet) (August 20, 2026): Based on Kimi K3 and trained with Fireworks on synthetic, public legal, and human-expert data, with no customer data. Presented as a research preview.
- [Review Table](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-frontier-review-table-models-with-applied-compute): GLM-5.2, trained with Applied Compute, where lawyers ask questions across documents and get a grid of answers with citations. [Applied Compute](https://www.appliedcompute.com/case-studies/harvey-review-table) describes the public legal documents, frontier-generated questions, and expert checks behind the training data.
- [Diligence](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence): With Baseten, a root agent divides a document room among sub-agents and assembles their findings into a diligence memo. Reinforcement learning on the Qwen3.5-122B-A10B root, with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B sub-agents held fixed.

**The result.**

- Tenet: Gains of nine percentage points on Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark and two on LAB Contracts in all-pass rate, meaning every evaluation criterion is satisfied.
- Review Table: A score of 0.903 (correctness and citation quality per cell, 0 to 1, at minimal reasoning effort), against 0.867 for Fable 5 and 0.857 for GPT-5.6-Sol. Average cost per cell is 54.8% below Sonnet 5.
- Diligence: The fraction of evaluation criteria passed rises from 29.9% to 63.0% on 50 synthetic lab diligence rooms held out of training.

These are research results. None of them establishes customer traffic on these checkpoints, or publicly downloadable weights, a public license, or a public model endpoint.

Harvey's [earlier work with OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harvey/) shows why a comparison needs refreshing. Lawyers at ten firms preferred a custom case-law model over GPT-4 97% of the time in side-by-side comparisons, though longer, more complete answers were among the reasons, so that number measures preference. Then, in a [May 2025 BigLaw Bench comparison](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/expanding-harveys-model-offerings), Harvey reports newer models optimized within its systems surpassing its originally benchmarked system. Specialization still needs a fresh comparison when the alternatives change.

## The job description

I'd start with the work that repeats: what comes in, what should come back, what makes an answer unusable, and who deals with it when it's wrong. That gives the evaluation a shape before a model has been chosen.

Then compare the existing workflow with the proposed one. Keep the ambiguous cases in the examples, measure the outcome the people doing the work care about, and count training, evaluation, engineering, monitoring, and serving alongside whatever still goes to a frontier model.

These are selected company reports, so they can't tell me the typical return or how many other attempts failed. They give me a better place to start the next conversation.

On the drive to the birthday party, I was asking how to replace frontier models. Now I'd start with a different question: what's the job? "Open source" is an applicant. The job description comes first.
